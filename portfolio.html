Background

The best way to understand a system
is to build it yourself.

What draws me in healthcare engineering is the cross-disciplinary, slightly messy kind of problem: the kind where you have to understand several systems at once — mechanical, optical, software, regulatory — before the right answer shows up, and where you can never lose sight of who you're building for and what it actually does for them.

That instinct got tested early. In undergrad, a class paired my team with a woman living with a vision disorder. She couldn't tell the buttons apart on her cooking machine, so we set out to build something impressive: connect it to an app, re-engineer the whole thing. She was confused. When we finally stopped pitching and started listening, the real fix was almost embarrassingly simple. We changed the buttons she already used so her fingers could distinguish them. That stuck with me. The job isn't to build the most advanced thing; it's to understand the real problem and solve it in the simplest way that works.

I came up through R&D and engineering, and I've never liked the split between the people who define the work and the people who build it. I do both. I write the code, model the CAD, build the system, and keep up with the AI tools as they move, getting my hands dirty with new ones all the time.
